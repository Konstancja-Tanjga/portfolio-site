# How I write: the rules

These rules govern every text that carries my name to other people: this
portfolio and its case studies, my CV, my LinkedIn posts, and my LinkedIn
profile. Anything new on those channels is written against this file, and
anything old is edited against it.

The grammar, punctuation and capitalisation rules are taken from NASA
SP-7084, *Grammar, Punctuation, and Capitalization: A Handbook for Technical
Writers and Editors* (Mary K. McCaskill, Langley Research Center, 1990;
[NTRS 19900017394](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19900017394)). Section
numbers in parentheses point into that handbook. Where the handbook says a
point is a matter of style and gives Langley's preference, the choice made
for these channels is stated here, and the few places where these channels
depart from Langley are listed at the end.

The handbook is written for research reports. Two things carry over
unchanged: a rule has a functional basis (it exists to make meaning clear),
and consistency within one document matters more than which authority is
followed (3.1, 4.1).

## 0. First principles

Before the grammar, four questions decide what gets written at all.

1. **Who reads this, and what do they need to decide?** A hiring manager
   gives a page thirty seconds. A LinkedIn reader gives a post one line. The
   text is built for that reader's decision, not for completeness.
2. **What is the one thing they must take away?** It goes first: in the
   title, the first sentence, the caption. Everything else supports it or is
   cut.
3. **Is every claim traceable to a fact?** A number has a source. A result
   that was not measured is not stated. A role is described as it was.
4. **Can it be said with fewer words?** Brevity is not the goal; conciseness
   is (2.4). A shorter text that loses the emphasis or the meaning is worse.

## 1. Grammar (handbook chapter 1)

**Nouns and possessives (1.2)**

- Singular or plural noun not ending in *s*: add *'s*. Noun ending in *s*:
  add the apostrophe only (*Jones'*, *users'*).
- Joint possession: *'s* on the last element (*Figma and Storybook's role*).
  Individual possession: *'s* on each.
- Inanimate nouns: prefer no possessive at all (*component API*, *team
  decisions*), not *component's API*. *'s* is acceptable when the object has
  lifelike qualities (*the system's behaviour*).

**Pronouns (1.3)**

- Every pronoun has a clear antecedent that agrees in number. Check each
  one.
- No broad reference: *this* and *which* do not refer to the idea of the
  previous sentence. Name the thing (*This rule changed...*, not *This
  changed...*) or delete the pronoun.
- First person is allowed and often right. *I* for my decisions. *We* only
  when it means *I and the team*, and the team has been named.
- *Who* for persons. *Which* in nonrestrictive clauses, enclosed by commas.
  *That* in restrictive clauses, no commas. *Which* after a preposition.
- Do not drop *that* after *believe*, *show*, *found*, *provided* (*The test
  showed that...*).
- Avoid the masculine pronoun for an unknown person: make the antecedent
  plural (*Planners see their rows*) or recast.

**Verbs (1.4)**

- Tense follows the kind of statement, and stays consistent inside it:
  narration of what happened is past; description of what exists and
  explanation of why are present; conclusions that hold in general are
  present. A case study therefore reads: what I did (past), what the product
  is (present), what it means (present).
- A subordinate verb after a past main verb is past, unless it states a
  general truth (*Research showed that users read the first row first*).
- Subjunctive for a recommendation or a condition contrary to fact (*I
  recommended that the label be moved*; *If the data were complete*). Use it
  only to signal real doubt.
- Active voice by default. Passive is right in five cases: the actor is
  unknown or unimportant, the receiver should be emphasised, the active
  sentence is abrupt, variety is needed, or a weak imperative is wanted.
- Verb agrees with its subject, not with a phrase in between (*The set of
  rules governs...*). *Data* is plural. Subjects joined by *and* take a plural
  verb; by *or*, the verb follows the nearer subject. A quantity treated as
  one measure takes a singular verb (*Twenty components was the first
  release*).
- Do not drop the auxiliary in a compound passive when the subjects change
  number (*the frame was approved and the tokens were published*).

**Adjectives and unit modifiers (1.5)**

- *A* before a sounded consonant, *an* before a vowel sound, judged by how an
  acronym is read (*an SRS reference*, *a NASA report*).
- Repeat the article for coordinate adjectives that name different things
  (*the desktop and the mobile layout*, two layouts).
- Hyphenate a unit modifier when it contains a participle (*flight-tested
  model*, *user-facing label*), a number (*three-column grid*,
  *56-pixel rail*), an implied connective (*design-code gap*), or a colour
  pair. Do not hyphenate after an *-ly* adverb, a comparative, a proper
  name, a foreign phrase, or when it is a predicate (*the model was flight
  tested*).
- Before hyphenating a long modifier, try a prepositional phrase: *the
  rail of 56 pixels* beats *the 56-pixel-wide navigation-rail component*.
- A measurement does not modify what it measures: *an altitude of 15 000 ft*,
  not *15 000-ft altitude*.

**Adverbs (1.6)**

- *Only*, *almost*, *nearly*, *also*, *merely* sit next to the word they
  modify (*valid only for*, not *only valid for*).
- No squinting adverbs: an adverb must clearly belong to one side.
- Split an infinitive when not splitting it would be ambiguous or awkward.
  Do not split one with a phrase.

**Prepositions and conjunctions (1.7, 1.8)**

- Idioms: *different from*, *similar to*, *compare with*, *correlation with*,
  *analogous to*.
- A sentence may end with a preposition if rewording would sound pompous.
- Repeat a preposition in a coordinate pair only when clarity or
  parallelism needs it.
- *If* for a condition; *whether* for alternatives. *Where* only for place.
  *While* only for time, or with care. *As* and *since* only where they
  cannot be read as time.
- Correlatives (*both...and*, *not only...but also*, *either...or*) are
  followed by the same part of speech on both sides.
- *However*, *therefore*, *thus* between independent clauses need a
  semicolon or a period before them, never a comma.

**Verbals (1.9)**

- An introductory gerund or infinitive phrase modifies the subject
  (*Using the prototype, I...*, not *Using the prototype, the question...*).
- No dangling participles: *Encouraged by the results, we began...*, not
  *Encouraged by the results, a new effort was begun*.
- A series of coordinate gerunds that is one process takes a singular verb
  and does not repeat the preposition.

## 2. Sentence structure (handbook chapter 2)

- **Subject and verb carry the sentence (2.2).** The subject is a concrete
  thing; the real action is the verb. *The jet lift coefficients indicate...*,
  not *An indication... is shown by...*.
- **No indirect openings.** *There is*, *there are*, *it is believed that*,
  *it appears that* waste the subject position. *Obviously a...*; *We
  believe that...*.
- **Vigorous verbs.** The action stays a verb, not a noun: *are compared*,
  not *a comparison is presented*; *agreed to within 30 percent*, not *the
  agreement was within 30 percent*; *differ*, not *are different*.
- **No hedges** (*tend to*, *appear to*, *seem to*, *may*, *might*) unless
  doubt is the point. **No empty intensifiers** (*very unique*, *quite
  impossible*, *more dominant*).
- **Keep subject and verb together.** A long modifier between them is
  shortened, moved, or made its own sentence.
- **Parallelism (2.3).** Logically equal ideas take the same grammatical
  form. Every item in a list is the same construction, and the lead-in
  reads into each item.
- **Wordiness (2.4.1).** Cut on sight:

  | Type | Example | Write |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | Tautology | *close proximity*, *in the range of 1 to 10* | *near*, *from 1 to 10* |
  | Dilute verb | *make adjustments to*, *give consideration to*, *take measurements of* | *adjust*, *consider*, *measure* |
  | Hiccup | *enter into*, *inside of*, *go on with* | *enter*, *inside*, *continue* |
  | Roundabout | *There are three characteristics in these screens* | *These screens show three characteristics* |
  | Pointless phrase | *It is interesting to note that*, *in the case when* | delete |
  | False elegance | *due to the fact that*, *with the exception of*, *a majority of* | *because*, *except*, *most* |

- **Shortening (2.4.2).** Subordinate, turn clauses into phrases and phrases
  into single words, combine short sentences that waste words, and drop
  *An investigation of*, *An overview of* from titles. Replace vague title
  words (*method*, *system*, *approach*) with what the thing is.
- **Comparisons (2.5).** Both items are named, and the standard is stated:
  *tougher than the control*, not *tougher*; *lower than those on the
  right*, not *lower than the right*. *Compare with*. *Different from*. *As
  hard as, if not harder than*. *The lower the temperature, the more brittle
  the steel.* Delete a superfluous *different*.
- **Emphasis (2.6).** The beginning and the end of a sentence are the
  emphatic positions. A short sentence after long ones is stressed; put an
  important idea there. *However* inside a sentence stresses the words
  before it. A colon stresses the list it introduces; a dash stresses what it
  encloses; parentheses de-emphasise.

## 3. Punctuation (handbook chapter 3)

Open style: punctuate to prevent misreading, not everywhere grammar allows
(3.1). Decisions that are a matter of choice are made once and kept.

- **Apostrophe (3.2).** Possessives as in section 1. Plurals of acronyms and
  years take no apostrophe (*PRs*, *1970s*). See the departures list.
- **Colon (3.4).** Only after a complete sentence. Never between a verb or
  preposition and its object (*The parts are a, b and c*, no colon), and
  never after *such as*, *for example*, *that is*. A colon may join two
  clauses when the second restates or amplifies the first. Hours:minutes,
  ratios.
- **Comma (3.5).**
  - Serial comma before the conjunction in a series: *rail, panel, and
    content*.
  - After an introductory clause and after an introductory phrase that
    contains a verb form. Optional after a short adverbial phrase. Never
    between an introductory phrase and the verb it modifies.
  - Nonrestrictive elements are enclosed by a pair of commas; restrictive
    ones take none. A comma that encloses needs a partner.
  - Between independent clauses joined by *and*, *but*, *or*; omitted when
    both clauses are short. Never between clauses with no conjunction.
  - Not between the two halves of a compound predicate.
  - Between coordinate adjectives only (test: *and* fits between them;
    their order can be swapped). When in doubt, no comma.
  - Dates with a day: *January 15, 1975, to...*; without: *January 1975 to*.
  - Thousands: a thin space, not a comma (*88 000*), because a comma is a
    decimal point in German and Polish.
- **Em dash (3.6).** An emphatic mark used sparingly. It encloses an
  interrupting element that has internal commas or needs emphasis, and
  needs a partner. It is not a routine substitute for a comma, colon or
  semicolon, and is not the way to join two thoughts.
- **En dash (3.7).** Inclusive numbers and dates (*pp. 233–235*, *2023–2026*),
  never after *from* or *between*. Joins a two-word element in a unit
  modifier. Replaces a hyphen in all-capital text.
- **Hyphen (3.8).** Required after *all-*, *half-*, *self-*, *ex-*, *quasi-*;
  before a proper noun (*un-American*); to avoid a doubled vowel (except
  *co*, *de*, *pre*, *re*: *preexist*, *cooperate*) or a misreading
  (*re-cover*, *un-ionized*). Compounds in the dictionary keep the
  dictionary's form (*state of the art*, noun; *state-of-the-art*,
  adjective). Unit modifiers as in section 1.
- **Italics (3.9).** For a word that would be stressed if spoken, rarely, and
  never a passage: recast the sentence instead. For a key term on first use.
  For a word used as a word. For titles of books, reports and periodicals.
  Not for jargon (use quotation marks, first use only).
- **Parentheses (3.10).** For a loosely connected aside. A parenthesised
  sentence inside another starts lowercase and takes no period.
- **Period (3.11).** None after a heading on its own line, a table title, or
  a list item that is not a sentence. Every figure caption ends with a
  period. No periods in acronyms or unit abbreviations.
- **Question mark (3.13).** Only after a direct question. A direct question
  inside a sentence is set off by a comma and may start with a capital.
- **Quotation marks (3.14).** Verbatim quotes, with the source clear. A term
  being defined or a coined term, first use only. Not around jargon the
  reader knows. Periods and commas go inside; semicolons and colons outside;
  other marks by sense. Titles of sections and articles.
- **Semicolon (3.15).** Between independent clauses with no conjunction;
  before *however*, *therefore*, *thus*, *that is*, *for example* when a
  clause follows; between items of a series that have internal commas. It
  slows the pace. Prefer a period in general writing.
- **Slash (3.16).** Only in *and/or*, fractions and *per*. Not to join
  alternatives or make a compound: write *and*, *or*, or hyphenate
  (*lateral-directional*, not *lateral/directional*).

## 4. Capitalisation (handbook chapter 4)

Down style: fewer capitals (4.1).

- **Sentence style** (first letter and proper nouns) for captions, table
  entries, list items, labels on figures, and, on these channels, for all
  headings. Items in a displayed list start with a capital whether or not
  they are sentences.
- **Headline style**, where it is used (document titles, award names as
  given): capitalise the first and last word, every word of four or more
  letters, both parts of a hyphenated pair, and the infinitive *to*; leave
  *a*, *an*, *the*, *at*, *by*, *for*, *of*, *in*, *up*, *on*, *to*, *and*,
  *as*, *but*, *if*, *or*, *nor* lowercase; unit abbreviations stay
  lowercase.
- **Acronyms** are full capitals, defined on first use, and the definition
  is lowercase unless it is a proper name (*what you see is what you get
  (WYSIWYG)*; *National Aero-Space Plane (NASP) Program*).
- **Proper nouns:** products and tools as their owners spell them (APplus,
  FOX, Big Hat, Figma, Storybook, Chromatic, Nexus, Dev Mode, Code Connect,
  Claude Code). A common noun in a proper name is capitalised in the name
  and lowercase alone (*the Best Practice Hub*; *the hub*). *The* is
  capitalised only when part of an official name.
- **Titles** are capitalised before a name (*Lead Designer Konstancja
  Tanjga-Nawrot*) and lowercase after it or alone (*Konstancja
  Tanjga-Nawrot, lead designer*; *the lead designer*). A CV headline or a
  LinkedIn job title field is a label and may carry the title as the
  employer writes it.
- **Designations** with a number or letter are lowercase: *chapter 4*,
  *figure 1*, *rule R2*, *version 9*.
- **Months and days** capitalised; **seasons** and **time zones** not.
- **Programme and award names** as official: *ERP-System des Jahres 2026*,
  *Gold in the User Experience category* (the category name is the
  organiser's).
- **Trade names** are used as adjectives and are never verbs.

## 5. The channels

**Portfolio and case studies.** A case study is narration (past) with
description and exposition (present) and a concluding section in which
results are past and conclusions present. Headings in sentence style. Every
caption names what is shown and the decision it demonstrates, in one or two
sentences, ending with a period. No caption begins *Screenshot of*. No
marketing adjectives (*beautiful*, *seamless*, *intuitive*, *powerful*); the
screens earn them silently.

**CV.** Fragments are allowed in a CV and start with a capital; they take no
period unless they are sentences. Entries under one heading are parallel:
all verb phrases (*Designed...*, *Led...*, *Shipped...*) or all noun phrases,
never mixed. Past tense for past roles, present for the current one. Every
number has a source I can name.

**LinkedIn posts.** One idea per post, in the first sentence. Short sentences,
one thought each. No hedges and no intensifiers. No slash compounds, no
all-capital words for emphasis, no exclamation points. A post about work
names what was decided and what it cost, not what was *exciting*.

**LinkedIn profile.** The About section follows the case study rules in
miniature: present tense for what I do, past for what I did, first person.
Job titles as the employer writes them. Skills as nouns.

## 6. Checklist before anything is published

1. Who reads it, what do they decide, and is the one thing they need in the
   first line?
2. Every pronoun has a clear antecedent; no *this* or *which* pointing at a
   whole sentence.
3. Every sentence has a concrete subject and a real verb; no *there is*, no
   *it is believed*.
4. No hedges, no intensifiers, nothing from the wordiness table.
5. Tense is consistent within each kind of statement.
6. Lists are parallel and the lead-in reads into every item.
7. Commas: serial comma present; nonrestrictive elements enclosed by a
   pair; no comma splice; no comma in a compound predicate.
8. Dashes rare and paired; en dash for ranges; no slashes outside
   *and/or*, fractions and *per*.
9. Sentence-style headings and captions; captions end with a period; list
   items and headings do not.
10. Every number has a source; every product name is spelled as its owner
    spells it; the spelling is British throughout.

## Departures from the handbook, and why

- **British spelling** (*colour*, *organisation*, *visualisation*). The
  handbook is American. The site was written in British English from the
  start and consistency wins (4.1).
- **Plurals of acronyms and years without an apostrophe** (*PRs*, *1990s*).
  Langley prefers *'s* (3.2); the handbook itself notes that Chicago uses
  the apostrophe only to avoid confusion, and on the web the apostrophe
  reads as a possessive.
- **Sentence-style headings everywhere.** Langley sets displayed headings in
  headline style (4.3). On a website and on LinkedIn, sentence style is the
  established convention and reads as less shouted; the rule that matters
  is one style throughout.
- **Thin space in thousands.** Kept from the handbook (3.5.3) because the
  readers are German and Polish, where the comma is the decimal mark.
