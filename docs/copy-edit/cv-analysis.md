# The CV of a lead designer: analysis and proposal

Date: 10 October 2026. Source: `sources/cv-2026-10.md` (the October PDF),
the portfolio on `main`, the LinkedIn Experience text, and the market
reading listed at the end. Written against [STYLE.md](../STYLE.md).

## 1. What the CV has to do

Three readers, in order, and the CV serves all three or it fails:

1. **A parser, in a second.** Applicant tracking systems (eRecruiter and
   Tellent both appear in the portfolio's referrers) read the text in document
   order. A two-column layout whose text order is scrambled loses the
   headline and the dates.
2. **A recruiter, in ten seconds.** Title, current employer, years, the one
   number that proves the level, the portfolio link. Above the fold of page
   one.
3. **A hiring manager, in two minutes.** What the candidate owned, what they
   decided, what it produced, how they led, and whether the claims hold up
   against the portfolio. For a lead role: scope of ownership, judgment under
   constraint, and work after handoff, not a process tour.

The research agrees on what gets skipped: "led end-to-end design" and other
competency language, methodology, and anything the candidate could not
defend under questioning. What gets read: the problem, the constraint, the
decision, the outcome, and who the candidate was to the team.

## 2. The current CV, section by section

**Header and headline.** Clear, one line, the right three links. The title
*Lead Product Designer* is one of three titles in circulation: the site's
About says *Lead Designer / UX Engineer*, LinkedIn says *Lead Designer*, the
case covers say *Lead Product Designer*. A hiring manager who checks will
notice. One title everywhere.

**Profile.** Good: a concrete first sentence, the domain list, the award,
"four products from zero". Missing: the team (the word *lead* appears only
as a title), the 2026 award, and the thing that distinguishes this
candidate from every other enterprise designer: the design system is code
and she merges into it. "Four products" disagrees with the site's "three";
the fourth is Elly, which the site counts under AI. One number, used
everywhere.

**How I work.** Four numbered steps: discovery, Figma, prototype, handoff.
This is the section the research says hiring managers skip: it describes a
process every candidate has. The sentence under it ("the design system
lives in code as versioned packages...") is the strongest line on the page
and is buried as a footnote. Proposal: replace the four steps with *What I
own*, the scope-of-ownership block a lead-level reader looks for, and
promote that sentence into it.

**In short.** Three dense bullets. The second ("Design systems in four
settings") is the differentiator and should be visible from the headline
down. The first lists products; the third lists methods. Keep the first two,
fold the third into the experience bullets where the methods produced
something.

**Experience, Asseco.** The structure (one line of scope, then each product
as a sub-entry with its own dates) is unusual and good: it shows ownership
per product and how long each took. The bullets, though, mostly describe
what the product *is*; the decision and the outcome are present in only
two of them (the permission model; the Elly study). "Owned ten functional
areas" and "owned eleven functional areas" are competency language. What is
absent entirely: the team of two and what leading them means in practice;
the work after handoff (PR review, design QA, reconciling file and code),
which a rejection explicitly asked for; the research rhythm; the sign-off
mechanism. These are the lead-level facts and they belong on page one.

**Experience, earlier.** Volvo and Xecta are well written. Xecta's dates
(04/2020–07/2021) contradict LinkedIn (04/2021–07/2022). Deloitte is listed
as *Project Manager* while the bullet claims design ownership of the
wellbeing platform; both can be true, and the line should say so in that
order.

**Education.** Fine. The em dashes become commas (STYLE.md 3.6). Course
counts are a detail few readers need.

**Practice.** Ten rows. The research suggests six or seven, each skill
resurfacing in at least one bullet. *Collaboration* and *Domains* are
derivable from the experience section; *Craft* lists tools a lead is
assumed to have. Six rows: research, structure, design systems, delivery
and code, AI tooling, accessibility.

**Recognition.** Add 2026. Name the category as the organiser does.

**What is not there.** Big Hat as a public artefact with numbers (39
components, two token layers, MIT, Storybook link); the two Claude Code
skills published on GitHub; one line of who she is off screen. The
portfolio now opens with the watercolour and the birds; the CV should not
read as a different person.

**Layout.** Two pages with a label column on the left is readable and calm.
Two risks: the label column may scramble the text order for a parser (test
by copying the PDF text into a plain editor and reading it top to bottom),
and page one ends mid-employer. Keep the layout; order the content so page
one carries the current role complete.

## 4. What good looks like on the market, in one paragraph

A lead designer's CV in 2026 opens with ownership, not process: what the
candidate was accountable for, at what scale, with whom. Every bullet is
problem, decision, outcome, and at least one in three carries a number with
a source. Leadership is shown as enabling the team's work (standards,
review, critique, hiring), not as hoarding the craft. AI tooling is stated
specifically (which tool, for what, with what effect). The portfolio holds
three to five case studies of shipped, verifiable work, each readable by
headings alone: context, problem, role and scope, the decisions and what
was rejected, the outcome, and what the candidate would do differently.
Concept work, student work, process diagrams and "end-to-end" language are
cut. The CV and the portfolio tell one story with the same numbers.

## 7. The case-study structure the CV points at

The CV links three case studies. Each must be readable by headings alone in
thirty seconds and must say the same numbers as the CV. The structure the
Analytics rework defines applies to all three:

1. **In short**: context, problem, my role and scope, three decisions,
   outcome, period and team.
2. **Hero**: the product, large, one line.
3. **The problem and its constraints**: who, what they needed, what made it
   hard.
4. **The decision trail**: options considered, what was rejected and why,
   what it cost.
5. **The rules**: what governs the whole product, shown as an artefact.
6. **Evidence after handoff**: review, QA, reconciliation, with artefacts
   or an honest one-line note where an artefact is internal.
7. **Outcome, and what I would do differently**: the results that exist,
   the measurement plan, one real trade-off.

Chapters that are a process tour (what research methods exist, what a
double diamond is) are cut or folded into the decision they produced.

## Sources

- Leslie Yang, [What a hiring manager looks for in product design portfolios](https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in): 3–4 tailored case studies, "I" statements, 3–5 sentences per section, no Dribbble or Behance galleries.
- The Fountain Institute, [What actually gets senior product designers hired in 2026](https://www.thefountaininstitute.com/blog/senior-product-designers-hired): "show your trade-offs, not your process"; 500–800-word decision-focused case studies; cut concept work and process diagrams; AI proficiency as a senior signal.
- Verified Insider, [Do design leaders need portfolios](https://verifiedinsider.substack.com/p/do-design-leaders-need-portfolios): leadership shown as enabling the team's work, standards and review, not hoarding the craft.
- Tiny.cv, [Product designer resume guide 2026](https://tiny.cv/blog/product-designer-resume-guide): ownership statements first; 6–7 skill rows, each skill resurfacing in a bullet; three or four case links in the header; ATS-parseable single reading order.
- Maven, [How to build a design portfolio that gets you hired](https://maven.com/p/bb7a31/how-to-build-a-design-portfolio-that-gets-you-hired): problem, decisions, outcome, learning; the 10–20-second skim.
